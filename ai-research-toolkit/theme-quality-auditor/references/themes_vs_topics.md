# Themes and topic summaries

## The distinction

Braun & Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis defines a theme as a pattern of shared
meaning, underpinned by a central organising concept. The concept is what unites the
extracts. It is a claim about the data, interpretive, and something a reasonable reader
could dispute.

A **topic summary** (sometimes called a domain summary) collects what participants said
about a subject. It is organised by subject, not by meaning. The extracts under it can
disagree with each other entirely — some people loved onboarding, some hated it — and
the "theme" still holds, because all it asserts is that onboarding was discussed.

That is the test: **could the extracts under this heading contradict each other and the
heading still be true?** If yes, it is a topic summary.

## Why topic summaries are so common

1. **The discussion guide becomes the structure.** Each guide section turns into a
   "theme", and each theme summarises the answers to one question. This is the most
   common route, which is why guide mirroring is treated as a strong signal.
2. **Grouping is easier than interpreting.** Sorting sticky notes by subject is quick.
   Saying what a cluster *means* is the actual analysis.
3. **AI synthesis tools make it easy.** Clustering by semantic similarity groups
   extracts that are about the same thing, which is the definition of a topic summary.
   A tool can produce a dozen tidy topic summaries from a dataset in seconds, and they
   will look like themes.

## Name shapes

| Reads as a topic | Reads as a theme |
|---|---|
| Onboarding | People give up on setup before they reach anything they'd value |
| Views on the mobile app | The mobile app is where people check, not where they act |
| Challenges with approvals | Approvers treat the tool as a queue to clear, not a decision to make |
| Trust | People keep a private record because they don't trust the tool's totals |
| Admin pain points | Admins rebuild permissions by hand for every new hire |
| Getting started | People give up before the first report, not during setup |
| What do you think of the mobile app? | The mobile app is where people check, not where they act |
| Likes and dislikes | — (never a theme) |

A theme name can be long. It usually contains a verb, a contrast, or a "because".

The same test applies to affinity diagrams. Holtzblatt & Beyer's Contextual Design
writes affinity labels in the user's voice, as statements ("I don't know who approved
what"), not categories ("Audit history").

## Language the method rejects

**"Themes emerged."** Braun & Clarke are explicit that themes don't passively emerge
from data. The analyst builds them by making interpretive choices. "Emerged" hides those
choices and implies the result was sitting in the transcripts waiting to be found.
They reject "identified" and "discovered" for the same reason: both imply the themes
were there before the analysis. Write what you did instead: "we developed" or "we
constructed" (Braun & Clarke 2024, *Health Promotion International*,
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11132294/).

**Prevalence as justification.** "Seven of eight participants mentioned it" can be worth
reporting. It isn't what makes something a theme. Something mentioned once can be
central, and something mentioned by everyone can be trivial. That's why the audit
reports thin evidence as a separate note and never counts it toward a topic-summary
verdict.

## Negative and divergent cases

Ask whether the theme accounts for participants who said the opposite. If it only
holds because those cases were left out, name them or narrow the claim.

## When topic summaries are fine

Codebook approaches, including framework analysis and some applied policy research,
sometimes use domain summaries on purpose, to organise material across cases before or
instead of interpreting it. That's a legitimate choice, and `--approach codebook` exists
for it.

What isn't legitimate is producing topic summaries and presenting them as reflexive
thematic analysis, which is a common mistake in synthesis readouts.

## The question to ask

For any heading, ask what the extracts under it have in common apart from their subject.

If there's an answer, that answer is the theme, and the heading should say it.

If there isn't one, the extracts belong somewhere else or nowhere, and the heading was a
folder.
